PROBLEM 1 — Thermal control of a spacecraft
STEFAN–BOLTZMANN LAW

Spacecraft electronics generate heat that must be removed to prevent components from exceeding their operating
temperatures. On Earth, convection can transfer heat to the surrounding air; in space, this mechanism is essentially absent.
Radiator panels therefore reject internally generated heat mainly through thermal radiation.

Consider a satellite whose electronics continuously dissipate 500 W. A radiator panel has an
effective emissivity ε = 0.85 and operates at T = 300 K. For this simplified model, neglect radiation
received from the Sun and Earth.

(a) Calculate the power radiated per unit area of the panel.
(b) Determine the radiator area required to dissipate 500 W.
(c) Engineers redesign the system so that the radiator can operate at 330 K. Determine the new
required area and the percentage change in radiator area.
(d) Explain why increasing the operating temperature can reduce the mass of a spacecraft thermal-
control system, and why engineers cannot simply increase the temperature indefinitely.

PROBLEM 2 — Measuring the temperature of molten steel
WIEN'S DISPLACEMENT LAW

In steel manufacturing, temperatures can exceed 1500 °C, making conventional contact thermometers difficult to use.
Optical pyrometers determine temperature remotely from thermal radiation. The familiar change of heated metal from dull
red to orange and yellow is itself a consequence of the temperature dependence of the thermal spectrum.

During a manufacturing process, the maximum of the measured emission spectrum occurs at λmax
= 1.55 μm.

(a) Estimate the temperature of the steel in kelvin.
(b) At what wavelength would the emission maximum occur if the steel cooled to 1000 K?
(c) The human eye is sensitive approximately between 400 and 700 nm. Explain why very hot
objects become visibly incandescent even when the maximum of their thermal spectrum is still in
the infrared.

PROBLEM 3 — Finding heat losses with a thermal camera
THERMAL RADIATION

Thermal imaging is widely used in civil and mechanical engineering to identify insulation defects, thermal bridges and
overheated electrical components. Objects near room temperature emit most of their thermal radiation in the infrared,
which is why thermal cameras operate at wavelengths much longer than those detected by the human eye.

During an inspection, a well-insulated region of an external wall is at 15 °C while a thermal bridge is
at 25 °C. Assume both surfaces have emissivity ε = 0.90.

  (a) Calculate the radiant power emitted per unit area by each surface.
  (b) Determine the percentage increase in emitted power from the warmer region.
  (c) Calculate the peak emission wavelength of each region using Wien's law.
  (d) Many building-inspection cameras operate approximately in the 8–14 μm range. Explain why
  this is appropriate.
  (e) Why would an ordinary visible-light camera be unable to make the same temperature
 measurement?

PROBLEM 4 — UV flame detection in industrial safety

PHOTOELECTRIC EFFECT

Industrial burners, furnaces and gas turbines require reliable systems to verify that a flame is present. One technology used
for this purpose is the UV flame sensor, in which ultraviolet photons emitted by a flame strike a photosensitive cathode and
eject electrons through the photoelectric effect. Commercial UV flame sensors can be designed to respond to radiation
approximately in the 185–260 nm range while remaining insensitive to visible light, allowing flames to be detected even in
the presence of intense visible radiation from hot surfaces.


Consider a simplified UV flame detector whose photocathode requires a minimum photon energy
of φ = 4.8 eV to eject an electron. Ultraviolet radiation from a flame, with wavelength λ = 250 nm,
reaches the photocathode.

(a) Calculate the energy of the incident 250 nm photons in eV.
(b) Determine whether photoelectrons are emitted and, if so, calculate their maximum kinetic
energy.
(c) Calculate the maximum speed of the emitted electrons.
(d) Determine the longest wavelength of radiation capable of producing photoemission from the
photocathode. Compare your result with the upper end, approximately 260 nm, of the operating
range of a typical UV flame sensor.
(e) A hot surface inside the furnace also emits intense visible radiation at 650 nm. Calculate the
energy of these photons and determine whether they can produce photoelectrons.
(f) During operation, the intensity of the 650 nm radiation increases by a factor of 1000. Would this
cause photoemission? Explain your answer using the photon model of light.
(g) Explain why the existence of a threshold wavelength is technologically useful for detecting a
flame in an environment containing hot surfaces and intense visible radiation.

PROBLEM 5 — Photoelectric effect and UV detection
PHOTOELECTRIC EFFECT

The photoelectric effect is the emission of electrons from a material when electromagnetic radiation of sufficiently high
frequency strikes its surface. It could not be explained by the classical wave theory of light. Einstein explained it in 1905 by
treating light as photons with energy Eγ = hν. Photoelectric detection remains important in photomultipliers, spectroscopy
and ultraviolet instrumentation.

Ultraviolet radiation of wavelength λ = 250 nm is incident on a potassium surface with work function
φ = 2.2 eV.

  (a) Determine the energy of the incident photons in eV.
  (b) Calculate the maximum kinetic energy of the emitted electrons.
  (c) Determine the maximum speed of the emitted electrons.
  (d) Calculate the longest wavelength capable of producing photoemission from this surface.
  (e) Explain why increasing the intensity of light with a wavelength longer than this threshold does
  not cause photoemission.

PROBLEM 6 — Selecting a material for a photodetector
PHOTON ENERGY AND SEMICONDUCTOR BAND GAPS
Photodiodes convert light into electrical signals and are used in cameras, industrial sensors, medical instruments and optical
communication systems. In a simplified semiconductor picture, a photon can create an electron–hole pair only if its energy
is at least the semiconductor band-gap energy.

Silicon has a room-temperature band gap Eg = 1.12 eV.


  (a) Calculate the maximum photon wavelength that can, in principle, generate an electron–hole
  pair in silicon.
  (b) Determine the photon energy at the telecommunications wavelength λ = 1550 nm.
  (c) Can an ideal silicon photodiode detect these photons through direct band-to-band
  absorption? Justify your answer.
  (d) Germanium has E_g = 0.66 eV. Calculate its corresponding threshold wavelength.
  (e) Explain why the choice of semiconductor material depends strongly on the wavelength
  range of the detector.

PROBLEM 7 — Photons in fibre-optic communications
PLANCK RELATION
Modern telecommunications networks transmit information using laser pulses propagating
through optical fibres. A standard wavelength is around 1550 nm, where silica fibres have very low
attenuation. Although ordinary optical pulses contain enormous numbers of photons, their energy
is ultimately built from discrete photon energies.

A fibre-optic transmitter operates at λ = 1550 nm with optical power P = 1.0 mW.

  (a) Calculate the energy of one photon.
  (b) Express the photon energy in eV.
  (c) Estimate the number of photons emitted per second.
  (d) Suppose information is transmitted using 1.0 ns optical pulses with the same instantaneous
  optical power. Approximately how many photons are in each pulse?
  (e) Explain why the discrete nature of light becomes increasingly important as optical signals
 become weaker.

PROBLEM 8 — Electron microscopy and nanotechnology

The spatial resolution of an optical microscope is fundamentally limited by the wavelength of

particle with momentum p has wavelength λ = h/p.

PROBLEM 9 — Electron-beam lithography
DE BROGLIE WAVELENGTH

Modern semiconductor devices contain structures with dimensions of only a few nanometres. Electron-beam lithography
creates small patterns by focusing energetic electrons onto an electron-sensitive resist. The very short de Broglie wavelength
of the electrons is one reason electron beams can define structures far smaller than the wavelength of visible light.

A lithography system operates with electrons of kinetic energy K = 20 keV. Use a non-relativistic
treatment.

  (a) Calculate the momentum of an electron.
  (b) Calculate its de Broglie wavelength.
  (c) Compare this wavelength with a feature size of 10 nm.
  (d)  If the kinetic energy is doubled to 40 keV, by what factor does the de Broglie wavelength
 change?
  (e) Why is the smallest feature produced by the instrument nevertheless much larger than the
  calculated wavelength?

PROBLEM 10 — Infrared sensors for autonomous systems
PLANCK DISTRIBUTION AND WIEN'S LAW
Infrared cameras can detect objects even when visible illumination is poor. Long-wave infrared
sensors used in surveillance, robotics and some automotive systems operate near 8–14 μm, where
objects around ambient temperature emit strongly.

Consider a pedestrian with effective surface temperature T = 305 K and surroundings at T_env =
285 K.

  (a) Determine the wavelength of maximum thermal emission from the pedestrian.
  (b) Repeat for the surroundings.
  (c) Calculate the blackbody radiant exitance for both temperatures.
  (d) Calculate the difference in emitted power per unit area.
(e) Explain why an infrared detector can distinguish the pedestrian from cooler surroundings at
  night.

PROBLEM 11 — Solar energy: photons arriving at a photovoltaic panel
PHOTONS AND POWER

Solar photovoltaic technology converts part of the energy carried by sunlight into electrical energy. The solar spectrum
contains photons over a wide range of wavelengths, and whether a photon can contribute to electricity generation depends
on both its energy and the electronic structure of the photovoltaic material.

For a deliberately simplified estimate, monochromatic light of wavelength λ = 550 nm delivers an
irradiance I = 1000 W m⁻² to a photovoltaic panel of area A = 1.5 m².

  (a) Calculate the energy of one 550 nm photon.
  (b) Calculate the total radiant power incident on the panel.
  (c) Estimate the number of photons striking the panel each second.
  (d) A silicon solar cell has band gap E_g = 1.12 eV. Compare this with the energy of a 550 nm
  photon.
  (e) In a simple picture, energy above the band gap is eventually lost as heat. Calculate the
  excess photon energy.
  (f) Explain why a semiconductor with an extremely small band gap would not automatically
 produce a highly efficient solar cell.

PROBLEM 12 — From a hot filament to an LED
THERMAL RADIATION VERSUS QUANTUM LIGHT SOURCES
Traditional incandescent lamps produce light by heating a tungsten filament to roughly 2700–3000
K. Most of their radiation is emitted in the infrared. LEDs use a different mechanism: electron–hole
recombination in a semiconductor produces photons with energies related to electronic energy
differences.   This    is  one   reason  LEDs  can  be  much  more   energy   efficient.

Consider a tungsten filament at T = 2800 K, a blue LED emitting at 450 nm and a red LED emitting
at 630 nm.

  (a) Use Wien's law to determine the wavelength at which the filament spectrum has maximum
  intensity.
  (b) Is this wavelength in the visible region?
  (c) Calculate the energy of a blue LED photon in eV.
  (d) Calculate the energy of a red LED photon in eV.
  (e) Which LED requires the larger electronic energy transition?
  (f) Using your answer to part (a), explain why incandescent lamps waste much more electrical
  energy as heat than LEDs.
  (g) Explain briefly the physical distinction between thermal emission from tungsten and photon
  emission associated with electronic transitions in a semiconductor.
